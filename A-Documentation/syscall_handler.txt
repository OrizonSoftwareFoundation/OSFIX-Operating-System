                Syscall Handling

One of the many things a modern OS has is ring 3, more commonly known as userspace, which is a separation of privileges
from kernel to user applications.
The means of communication from ring 3 applications to the kernel and kernelspace drivers is via defined system calls.
Syscalls differ from OS to OS, some may follow the POSIX/UNiX standard (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POSIX), 
others may follow the NT standard, and other others might just make their own standard out of spite or "uniqueness".

Fortunately for me, i've decided to follow in the footsteps of the UNiX and Linux because i do NOT feel like creating
my own standard and translating everything, as i'm not a masochist. You see, the reason why i followed the POSIX compatibility
is because, well, unlike some normies, i want my OS to actually run standard applications from both UNiX and Linux natively. 

Now, let's start with a little Q&A, shall we?

Q: What are rings?
A: Rings are the separation from the kernel to userspace apps. The kernel resides in ring 0, 
while most applications reside in ring 3. See to the diagram below:

        Ring 0 
(Kernel & kernelspace drivers)
          |
          v
    Ring 1 (unused)
          |
          v
    Ring 2 (unused)
          |
          v
        Ring 3 
(Userspace applications)

The reason why rings 1 & 2 exist was because back in the day, kernels that did not want their drivers residing in the kernel
often opted into just putting the drivers separate of the kernel, and having the drivers communicate via pathways made specifically
for said drivers in different privilege levels. Nowadays, they mainly sit unused because the method microkernels use is inefficient, 
since talking to drivers in a different ring is slow compared to talking to drivers in the same ring via functions and whatnot.

You might be screaming at me behind your spit-filled monitor, "yes but how do your syscalls work you time waster?", and to that i say: look below.

On x86_64, we use the "syscall" instruction. To put simply: It's a single CPU instruction that, once ran, does the following events:

1: If in ring 3, switch from it back to ring 0
2: Jump to a kernel entry point (which is already in an MSR)
3: Saves the return address in "rcx" and flags in "r11"

Me personally? I chose to use the Linux System V ABI for calling convention (because i'm not a normie and like my shit to work)

As seen in kernel/sys/includes/syscall.h, the syscalls are declared in a defined list called "SYSCALL_LIST" (i know, very creative name)
After the syscalls are declared, the list does something equivalent to "extern int64_t sys_*(void);" and ships it off to wherever they're
needed.

Now, the actual syscalls are declared in kernel/sys/sys_proc.c and the external facing declarations are in kernel/sys/includes/sys_proc.h.
Basically, the syscall functions are declared in sys_proc.c/.h, then the actual syscalls that userspace applications talk to are declared in
syscall.c/.h and given to syscall_asm.asm in kernel/sys/includes/asm to ship to any userspace applications that are calling specific syscall 
numbers, because as you all know, syscalls are NUMBERED!!!

So, if the applications call syscall 1 for example, then the assembly syscall handler will see which syscall in the C declaration matches the 
number, pick it out, and hand back the result from the syscall.
